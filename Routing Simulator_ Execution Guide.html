Routing simulator: how the project fits together

You build a small internet in Python, let two routing methods fight over it, then check your results against real routers.

Watch distance-vector break

Three routers. Y and Z both reach X. Y's direct link to X costs 4, Z's direct link costs 50, and Y–Z costs 1. Then the Y–X link jumps to 60. Each round, every router sends its distance to X to its neighbour and recomputes. Step through it, then turn on poisoned reverse and run it again.

Without poisoned reverse, Y trusts Z's old news and Z trusts Y's, so the numbers climb by two every couple of rounds until Z's direct 50 finally wins. With it, Z tells Y "I can't reach X" whenever Z's route goes through Y, so Y never falls for the loop. The correct end state is Y 51, Z 50. Your simulator has to reproduce both runs and count the rounds and messages.

The two halves of the project

Routers never read global state. A router knows its own links and talks only by sending messages over them. That is what makes the simulator a true distributed protocol, and it is the one rule you cannot bend.

Link-state (OSPF-like)

Each router floods a sequence-numbered advertisement of its links. Everyone ends up with the same map and runs Dijkstra locally. Fast and loop-free, but it costs more messages and memory.

Distance-vector (RIP-like)

Each router tells neighbours its distance to every destination and applies Bellman-Ford. Simple and cheap, but slow to react to bad news and prone to count-to-infinity.

What gets measured

Convergence time and rounds, messages and bytes, how long count-to-infinity lasts, how well your costs match FRR, and how all of it scales at 10, 50 and 200 nodes.

Five weeks

  • Week 1: contract and setup

    Study OSPF and RIP behaviour, freeze the shared contract. Zaid starts the core and loader. Adnan and Atta get FRR and containerlab running, each with one router pair talking.

  • Week 2: protocols on fake loops

    Zaid adds link events. Adnan has flooding and Dijkstra working, plus the metrics hook. Atta has basic DV working. Each tests with a small fake message loop, so nobody waits on the engine.

  • Week 3: integration

    Zaid ships the random generator and the count-to-infinity script, then hands over the engine. Atta adds poisoned reverse. Both FRR labs can export routing tables.

  • Week 4: validation

    Adnan compares LS with OSPF, Atta compares DV with RIP. Atta starts the scalability runs. Zaid fixes engine bugs and cross-checks metrics.

  • Week 5: results and paperwork

    Finish plots, take the pcap captures, write the report and README, rehearse the demo. Optional extras only if everything else is done.

Where validation usually goes wrong

TrapWhat to do
RIP counts hops and treats 16 as infinityUse unit costs when comparing DV with RIP, and keep paths under 15 hops. Your own DV can use real costs for the other experiments.
OSPF uses configurable interface costsSet each FRR interface cost to match the simulator's link cost, or the tables will never agree.
Equal-cost pathsCompare total path cost, not the exact next hop. Two correct answers can differ.
Real timers are slowRIP updates every 30 seconds by default and OSPF delays its SPF runs. Note the timers you use and tune them down. Compare the shape of convergence, not raw seconds.
200 nodes in FRRNot realistic on a laptop. Validate on about 5 to 10 routers and use the large graphs for simulator scaling only.
Cherry-picked runsThe brief wants each metric under at least 3 conditions, at least 3 runs each, with mean and spread. Seed your random graphs and log the seed.
